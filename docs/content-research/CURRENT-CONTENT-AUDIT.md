# Current content audit: density before expansion

**Prepared 23 October 2026.** Author analysis of participant instrument `0.4.0` and repository snapshot `d0252e7`; proposed revisions only. The current application, scoring, translations, and saved-response meanings are unchanged. This is a desk review of authored content, not an independent expert panel, an observed response-pattern analysis, or completed cognitive interviews. All predicted ambiguities and response tendencies below are hypotheses to investigate. No source is treated as verified merely because it appears in the existing source queue.

The current route contains 24 value statements, 16 descriptive premises, eight main situations, and one repeated situation with a changed deadline. The 49 responses cover useful civic dilemmas, but breadth and length are not the same thing. The main opportunity is to replace repetitive or weakly specified items with distinctions that let readers express different explanations, obligations, and institutional preferences.

## What stronger density should mean

An item earns its place if it addresses a question not already adequately covered, states a claim a reader can interpret, and admits more than one intelligible response without relying on a flattering label. A situation earns its place if its options separate relevant priorities under stated constraints. Additional words should resolve a decision-relevant ambiguity; additional items should test a distinct idea or a deliberate replication. Neither more traditions in metadata nor a higher item count demonstrates better measurement.

Four weaknesses deserve priority before adding content:

1. **The existing four summaries combine distinct constructs.** Public financing is not public ownership; international assistance is not acceptance of international authority; transparency is not simply the opposite of public-order restrictions; recognizing previously excluded people is not equivalent to favoring every kind of cultural change.
2. **The cultural and diplomatic banks repeatedly ask closely related questions.** Repetition can be deliberate in a later scale, but at this stage it uses scarce attention while agency, authority, membership, care, knowledge, ecological limits, and structural power receive little or no situated examination.
3. **Several premises ask about possibilities or widely attractive principles.** “Can contain useful knowledge,” “may miss,” and revising an explanation when contradicted do not require choosing how much authority to give those ideas when they conflict with something else. Broad agreement would not itself show that the underlying debate is unimportant, but would limit the item's descriptive resolution in this use.
4. **Scenario options frequently bundle a political arrangement with different material outcomes.** This is realistic for a decision game, but it limits explanations of a response. Keep the outcome differences when the purpose is to study tradeoffs; use a separately drafted, controlled variant when the purpose is to distinguish a particular institutional commitment.

Recommendations use three editorial priorities: **A** = resolve the interpretation before stronger feedback or comparative claims; **B** = revise or compare alternatives in the next content round; **C** = retain as an anchor candidate and probe its limits. “Retain” means retain for review, not validated. “Retire” means remove from a prospective core form, while preserving the original wording and ID in the version history.

## The 16 current foundation statements

These are presently unscored, which is appropriate while their intended meanings remain exploratory. The proposed revisions must remain distinguishable from ideology labels. A person may reasonably accept agency and social formation, property as one safeguard and property as a source of dependence, or situated knowledge and public evidential scrutiny at the same time.

| Current ID and wording | Main issue to investigate | Proposed disposition and distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `premise-personal-agency`: “An individual can form political commitments that are independent of the social groups they belong to.” | “Can” asks whether one exceptional person is possible; “independent” could mean dissent, absence of influence, or ability to justify a belief. Avoid suggesting that relational accounts deny agency. |
| `premise-social-relations`: “A person’s political interests are formed through their relationships with other people.” | Political interests might mean stated preferences, objective advantage, identity, or solidarity. | `premise-economic-power`: “Control over productive resources is a central source of lasting political power.” | “Productive resources” and “central” are technical or elastic. | `premise-ecological-dependence`: “Human societies cannot be understood separately from the ecological systems that sustain them.” | May receive basic factual assent without distinguishing human welfare, ecological limits, or nonhuman moral standing. “Cannot be understood” is exceptionally broad. | **B — replace one broad sentence with separate candidate claims.** Keep material dependence, non-substitutability of ecosystems, and the standing of nonhuman life distinct; include a real cost in normative candidates. |

## The nine current situation instances

The explicit role powers, common feasibility assumptions, ability to skip or report no adequate choice, and exclusion of the housing repetition from totals are useful foundations. However, a reasonable choice under a role is not a direct reading of a political identity. The table addresses what each case can currently reveal and what its next content version should distinguish.

| Situation | What the current case can describe | Limitation and next content task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `housing-urgent` | Choice between a sale, municipal construction, and a cooperative lease while urgent school repairs compete for resources | The plans differ in number of homes, completion time, rent level, financing, ownership, and repair funding. A cooperative is a distinct governance arrangement rather than a theoretical economic midpoint. **A:** retain as a complex tradeoff case; describe the chosen bundle and reason. Draft a separate case with matched material outcomes if the target is ownership/control. |
| `housing-planned` | Whether a participant changes their selected bundle after the school-repair deadline changes | It is always second and explicitly recalls the first case. Recognition, consistency pressure, learning, or interpretation can influence change. **A:** retain as a descriptive reflection exercise; a causal urgency study requires a separate allocation design. Do not interpret change as inconsistency or a measured role effect. |
| `public-assembly` | Consultation preference among relocation, new time/sound conditions, and consistent use of existing rules | This is the only scored civil situation. Sleep burden, actual noise levels, likely effectiveness, enforcement cost, and message visibility remain partly open. The middle option might appear to solve both concerns without a clearly comparable cost. **B:** state the expected remaining disruption and visibility under each plan; distinguish protection of dissent from administrative predictability. |
| `water-partnership` | Allocation between local upgrades and an international partnership when neither district faces an emergency | Equal need and shared audit are useful. But output per unit, reciprocal learning benefit, and whether the international grant mostly assists others are not quantified. **B:** decide whether the construct is duties beyond borders or mutually beneficial coordination; make the relevant outcomes explicit. Do not use the same answer as evidence of both. |
| `community-festival` | Preference for preserving a funded tradition, reserving part of the program, or applying open selection criteria | “Open” is procedural fairness and does not guarantee new activities replace old ones. | `service-contract` | Choice of operational arrangement under equal baseline routes, fares, safety, budget, and review | Stronger control of material facts than housing. Still, an external operator need not be private and for-profit; staffing rights, ownership, exit, and expected oversight burdens are only partly specified. **B:** identify the operator type and managerial powers. Consider a distinct cooperative/worker-governed option rather than automatically treating mixed delivery as a midpoint. |
| `community-history` | How a voluntary association allocates one exhibition under a mission, budget, and member vote | Inclusion, representation, specialist mission, archive access, and curricular priorities are entangled. Use another dilemma to examine authority inside civil society, since this mostly repeats the festival theme. |
| `public-records` | Preference among request-based access, useful summaries, and proactive files under a fixed legal floor | Descriptive treatment is well justified. | `regional-cooperation` | A national proposal for domestic flood works versus a joint river program under institutional approvals | Cross-border prevention may benefit domestic residents more than domestic works; the current options do not establish comparable benefit or probability. ### What is missing from the situation layer

The current scenarios do not directly test most of the distinctive foundation premises. Adding more general propositions without situations that differentiate their implications would widen the theory vocabulary while leaving the game concentrated on public-service packages and cultural-program allocation.

Prioritize new draft situations that examine:

- **Power without actual mistreatment:** a benevolent employer or landlord has unilateral authority, versus a contestable arrangement with explicit costs. This separates interference, security, and domination.
- **Worker voice versus investor or public authority:** consequential participation rights with common affordability and service outcomes. This makes economic democracy visible apart from public ownership.
- **Care and dependency:** limited support for unpaid caregivers, care recipients' autonomy, and collective provision, with no assumption that families are universally willing or safe.
- **Inherited status and equal standing:** access to a role or resource under a durable social hierarchy; distinguish nominally equal procedures from remedies for barriers. Specific caste, racial, gender, or colonial histories require informed contextual review rather than interchangeable labels.
- **Knowledge and authority:** disagreement between standardized evidence and place-based observations, with an explicit uncertainty and a feasible way to investigate it. Technical expertise, affected people's testimony, and democratic authorization are separate inputs.
- **Ecological limits and future claims:** a real opportunity cost and effects on future residents or nonhuman life, without giving one option an unstated technological rescue.
- **Political membership and public authority:** duties to long-term residents, recent arrivals, citizens abroad, or a self-governing community; avoid assuming that citizenship, residence, ancestry, and consent coincide.
- **Institutional repair:** continuing current injustice versus a specified transition risk. This distinguishes reform pace from general risk aversion and tests whether authority remains contestable during change.

These are development targets, not a requirement to append eight more situations to every route. A reviewed bank and a shorter allocated form can offer more conceptual breadth than a single ever-longer questionnaire.

## Worked wording alternatives for review

The following are illustrative English candidates, not approved replacements or verified paraphrases of a named author. They intentionally narrow the claim. Each should receive independent source, viewpoint, and bilingual review before any production version. A pair can be accepted together; it is not automatically a bipolar scale.

| Target | Candidate wording | What it clarifies; what still needs review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public financing apart from delivery | “Taxes should cover a basic level of essential services for everyone, even if households with higher incomes pay more than the value they receive.” | Names a distributive cost without selecting the provider. “Basic” and the chosen service package remain to specify in a situation. |
| Delivery apart from financing | “When cost, access, and service standards are the same, public funding should allow people to choose among several providers.” | Isolates provider choice more closely. The practical meaning of equal standards, ability to switch, and effects on solidarity need probing. |
| Standing power | “A person is less free when their landlord can end their tenancy without giving a reason, even if the landlord has always treated them well.” | Makes the capacity to interfere distinct from actual interference. Lease terms, appeal, other housing, and what “less free” means need controlled context. |
| Property and private dependence | “Having secure access to housing should not depend entirely on an owner's willingness to keep providing it.” | Names dependence rather than only ownership by government. It does not settle whether a tenancy right, public housing, cooperative ownership, or a transfer achieves security. |
| Social formation without denying agency | “Changing a person's position in work or family life can change which political problems matter to them.” | Accessible, modest relational claim; probably insufficient by itself to distinguish stronger social ontologies. Use as an interview entry point, then investigate mechanisms. |
| Worker decision rights | “Workers should elect representatives with a binding vote on major workplace decisions, even when the enterprise's investors prefer to decide alone.” | Specifies consequential voice and a claimant conflict. “Major” decisions, share of votes, coverage of nonstandard workers, and justified investor interests need separate drafting. |
| Competing obligations | “When equally serious needs cannot all be met, a government has a stronger duty to its residents than to people living elsewhere.” | Matches need and identifies the duty bearer. Exceptions for responsibility for harm, treaty duties, and effects on residents must be separately examined. |
| Ecological moral standing | “Preventing the disappearance of a species can justify a public cost even when its loss is not expected to harm people.” | Separates nonhuman standing from dependence on useful ecosystems. Cost magnitude and uncertainty need a situation; a positive response does not establish a complete ecological philosophy. |
| Weight of inherited practice | “An established practice deserves some weight in public decisions even when its benefits cannot yet be fully explained.” | Makes an evidential presumption explicit instead of asking whether any tradition might contain knowledge. “Some weight” is still a low threshold; test a concrete conflict before retention. |
| Repair pace | “Ending an institution's continuing unfair treatment can justify changing it quickly, even when a gradual transition would be easier to manage.” | Brings the harm of delay into view. “Unfair” requires a stipulated, comprehensible case; otherwise readers supply different injustices and transition costs. |

The last two candidates illustrate why a revision is not automatically stronger. Lowering abstraction can improve comprehension while reducing conceptual reach; adding a cost can improve specificity while changing the construct. Keep both the rationale and the rejected wording so later reviewers can see those tradeoffs.

## Omissions in the current interpretive map

The current map has useful pluralist cautions, but the author labels still cover only a limited set of debates. The next content map should address the following without treating them as single uniform camps:

| Missing or thin question | Alternatives to represent without assigning respondents to a camp |
| --- | --- |
| What motivates cooperation? | Self-interest under rules; reciprocity; solidarity; moral commitment; identity and social recognition; dependence and vulnerability. |
| What legitimates authority? | Consent; democratic participation; competence; inherited obligation; religious reasons; order and continuity; effective contest; self-government. Distinguish an explanation of obedience from a justification of rule. |
| How is durable inequality reproduced? | Class and ownership; gendered care; caste; racialization; colonial relations; legal status; unequal capabilities; organizational and military power. These mechanisms can interact. |
| What gives membership its significance? | Residence and participation; citizenship; shared history; ancestry; cultural attachment; universal personhood; distinct peoples' self-determination. Membership and exclusion need separate questions. |
| Who has standing beyond present voters? | Future generations, noncitizen residents, people affected across borders, nonhuman beings, and particular communities with historical claims. |
| Which changes need justification? | Departure from inherited practices; continuation of unequal arrangements; coercive hierarchy; expansion of state power; changes to private control. Different burdens of justification can shape the same policy judgment. |
| How should disagreement be governed? | Voting, courts, deliberation, expertise, negotiated pluralism, decentralized exit, consensus, and organized dissent. No mechanism should stand for a whole political philosophy. |

Do not map a real-country inspiration onto one of these theories. Fictional institutions should provide decision-relevant constraints; explicit real-country correspondences remain confined to the final debrief. Source-informed theory coverage requires texts and informed critics from the relevant traditions, not assigning a new label to a statement already in the bank.

## Review order before any implementation

1. Agree on the narrower claims the next feedback may support. Treat the present four domains as provisional organizing headings; do not write new items solely to fill their existing poles.
2. Resolve the high-priority interpretation problems: transparency versus rights/order; economic ownership versus financing and access; cooperation versus international authority; inclusion versus cultural replacement; mixed or cooperative governance as a distinct arrangement.
3. Draft replacements for redundant statements before expanding the route. Preserve old IDs for archival comparison; meaning changes require new revision records rather than silently reusing old responses.
4. Develop a source-linked bank with explicit counterarguments and within-tradition disagreements. Use sources to establish the intended debate, not to certify the item's wording or predict who should agree.
5. Select a small set of linked situations that can reveal different reasons for the same action. Specify information, uncertainty, role powers, costs, and the alternative interpretation that each scenario is designed to distinguish.
6. Review English wording and Vietnamese conceptual equivalence, including obligation versus preference, public versus governmental, control versus ownership, and political interests versus personal benefit. The examples above are English editorial drafts, not a bilingual-ready form.
