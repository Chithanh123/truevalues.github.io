## Why political traditions need more than an endorsement list

For content design, distinguish at least five questions:

1. **Social ontology and explanation:** What actors, relations, and mechanisms matter? Individuals, classes, households, states, associations, inherited institutions, and ecological relations are different units of analysis. An explanatory claim also needs a stated scope; the account need not apply equally in every setting.
2. **Normative commitments:** What is owed to whom, and why? Equal standing, desert, need, liberty, belonging, care, sacred obligation, and ecological standing can conflict or coexist.
3. **Political knowledge:** What counts as adequate evidence? Who can identify a problem, and how can claims be challenged? Knowing about a problem does not automatically confer the authority to decide it.
4. **Institutional mechanisms:** How might an aim be realized? Property, markets, public provision, associations, commons, courts, elections, and worker control are arrangements with different powers and dependencies, rather than single moral positions.
5. **Judgment under responsibility:** Which action is justified within a particular mandate, with specified resources and consequences? The same person may favor a principle but be unable to implement their preferred arrangement in an assigned role.

These are working analytic distinctions. A statement about property may combine an entitlement claim with a prediction about independence. Mark that mixture; do not rename it pure ontology. Likewise, normative disagreement is not an error to be corrected by an empirical fact, although factual assumptions can influence its application.

The following map supplies **questions for review**, not definitive summaries of every adherent or predictions about item endorsement. Traditions overlap, and individuals can draw from several.

| Debate family | Content questions to preserve | Internal disagreement or necessary comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical and social liberal approaches | Is freedom primarily protected choice, or does it require the means to exercise choice? What makes coercion legitimate? | Market and property protections can coexist with disagreement over redistribution, social provision, and concentrated private power. Compare Mill, Rawls, and capabilities approaches; do not define liberalism solely by privatization. |
| Marxist and other socialist approaches | How do productive relations shape interests and political power? Who should control investment and work? | Class analysis is not identical to state ownership. Central planning, market socialism, democratic coordination, worker control, and abolition of domination need separate examination. |
| Social democracy | Which inequalities and dependencies can democratic institutions reduce within a mixed economy? | Universal services, redistribution, collective bargaining, ownership reform, and democratic participation do different work. It is not necessarily a numerical midpoint between markets and state ownership. |
| Conservative and communitarian approaches | What knowledge and obligations arise through institutions and membership? What is lost through abrupt change? | Prudence, inherited authority, national belonging, religious duty, and local association are not interchangeable. A conservative can endorse substantial repair to preserve an institution's function. |
| Republican approaches | When does the capacity to exercise uncontrolled power make someone unfree? What makes contestation effective? | Public and private domination both matter; electoral control, legal contestation, material independence, and participatory power can be rival remedies. This does not name a particular political party. |
| Anarchist and libertarian debates | Which hierarchies need justification? Can voluntary association coordinate common tasks? | Right-libertarian property claims, left-libertarian equality claims, and socialist anarchist critiques of ownership must not be merged into a single market pole. Cooperation does not imply a central state. |
| Feminist, care, and relational approaches | How are agency, dependency, labor, credibility, and public/private boundaries organized? | Care can support redistribution or paternalism depending on who decides and who bears the burden. Situated knowledge does not imply that a social group has one view or cannot be mistaken. |
| Ecological approaches | Are nature and future generations relevant only through present human interests? What limits legitimate development? | Stewardship, intrinsic standing, ecological justice, growth skepticism, and human-centered conservation differ. Ecological dependence as a fact does not establish any one environmental policy. |
| Anti-caste and anti-racist approaches | How can inherited status persist despite formally equal rules? What changes material or social standing? | Anti-discrimination, redistribution, representation, institutional transformation, and reparative measures are distinct proposals. Caste, race, and class cannot be used as interchangeable names. |
| Anti-colonial, decolonial, and specific Indigenous approaches | Who controls land, membership, knowledge, and self-determination? Which historical relations remain active? | National liberation, institutional recognition, redistribution, and relational obligations may diverge. One author or nation cannot represent all colonized or Indigenous peoples. |
| Religious and nonreligious public justification | Which reasons may authorize a law binding people who do not share a faith? | Religious reasoning, equal citizenship, state establishment, toleration, and legal coercion are separate questions. Religiosity alone cannot indicate authoritarianism, conservatism, or social solidarity. |
| Confucian and other relational political approaches | How do role obligations, cultivated judgment, and public authority relate? | Relational responsibility is not equivalent to obedience. Democratic, perfectionist, and meritocratic interpretations need separate review; a regional label cannot substitute for a tradition-specific account. |
| National and cosmopolitan membership | What special duties arise through membership, residence, or humanity? | Solidarity with co-members, citizenship rules, cultural exclusion, cross-border institutions, and duties to outsiders can move independently. Neither a country's label nor its inhabitants identify a position. |

The draft candidates cover parts of these debates, unevenly. In particular they do **not** yet substantively represent distinct Indigenous political ontologies, Black political thought, the range of Islamic or Christian political arguments, Confucian disputes, disability justice, gender and sexuality, or authoritarian justifications. Do not turn this map into a claim of coverage. Prioritize sources and informed reviewers for these gaps before adding a tradition badge.

## Proposed theory readings

Every row has status **proposed / not retrieved in this round**. Short descriptions are reading questions and editorial intentions. Edition and passage locators are deliberately pending. Read a work's argument, its qualifications, and serious criticism before linking a question to it. None is a validation study of this assessment.

| ID | Bibliographic proposal | What to investigate for content design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T01 | John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859) | Distinguish individuality, interference, harm, and social pressure; examine what the harm principle leaves contested. |
| T02 | John Rawls, *Political Liberalism* (1993) | Distinguish political justification among diverse citizens from a comprehensive account of the good; compare public reasons with reasons for personal action. |
| T03 | Karl Marx, *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, Preface (1859) | Examine the explanatory relationship among material production, social relations, and political life without converting it into a claim that individuals cannot dissent. |
| T04 | Friedrich A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945) | Examine dispersed knowledge and the proposed coordination mechanism; separately test whether the argument implies a conclusion about every public institution. |
| T05 | Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990) | Investigate user governance and rule systems beyond a public/private binary; examine the conditions and limits of an institutional account. |
| T06 | Philip Pettit, *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government* (1997) | Distinguish interference, arbitrary power, dependence, and contestability, including when no interference has occurred. |
| T07 | Isaiah Berlin, “Two Concepts of Liberty” (1958) | Examine different questions about liberty and the risks of collapsing them into one scale; avoid treating either concept as one party's exclusive property. |
| T08 | Elizabeth Anderson, “What Is the Point of Equality?” (1999) | Compare distributive and relational accounts of equality and the role of social standing; test whether an item concerns outcomes, relations, or both. |
| T09 | Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999) | Distinguish resources, actual opportunities, and agency; explore why a common income or service entitlement need not produce equal freedom. |
| T10 | Joan C. Tronto, *Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care* (1993) | Examine political responsibility for care, unequal care burdens, and who defines needs; avoid equating care only with private family duty. |
| T11 | Iris Marion Young, *Justice and the Politics of Difference* (1990) | Investigate structural constraints, group differences, and critiques of reducing justice to distribution. |
| T12 | Edmund Burke, *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) | Examine continuity, inheritance, authority, and political change in historical context; separate prudential judgments from the defense of every existing hierarchy. |
| T13 | Michael Oakeshott, *Rationalism in Politics and Other Essays* (1962) | Examine practical knowledge and political judgment; distinguish knowledge embedded in a practice from its moral justification. |
| T14 | Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) | Examine entitlement, transfer, rectification, and limits on state authority; do not reduce the account to a preference for more income inequality. |
| T15 | Peter Kropotkin, *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902) | Investigate cooperation and decentralized organization, distinguishing explanatory accounts of mutual aid from institutional prescriptions. |
| T16 | G. A. Cohen, *Why Not Socialism?* (2009) | Examine equality, community, incentives, and the feasibility problem; compare a normative argument with a prediction about motivation. |
| T17 | David Miller, *On Nationality* (1995) | Investigate national membership and special duties, distinguishing their justification from automatic exclusion of outsiders. |
| T18 | Kwame Anthony Appiah, *Cosmopolitanism: Ethics in a World of Strangers* (2006) | Examine obligations across difference alongside particular attachments; avoid defining cosmopolitanism as having no local loyalties. |
| T19 | B. R. Ambedkar, *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) | Investigate inherited hierarchy, social relations, and reform; test whether formal neutrality misses an active status structure. |
| T20 | Frantz Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961; English edition to be specified) | Examine colonial domination, liberation, and postcolonial authority; separate reported historical arguments from endorsement of a particular contemporary action. |
| T21 | Glen Sean Coulthard, *Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of Recognition* (2014) | Investigate recognition, land, dispossession, and self-determination in the author's specific context; not a proxy for all Indigenous thought. |
| T22 | Val Plumwood, *Feminism and the Mastery of Nature* (1993) | Examine human/nature divisions, dependency, and domination; separate ecological ontology from a preference for a particular conservation package. |
| T23 | Aldo Leopold, *A Sand County Almanac* (1949) | Investigate the scope of an ethical community and ecological obligations; compare ecological standing with consequences for human welfare. |
| T24 | Khaled Abou El Fadl, *Islam and the Challenge of Democracy* (2004) | Investigate religious interpretation, authority, pluralism, and democracy; read the debate and distinguish author from respondents. |
| T25 | Joseph Chan, *Confucian Perfectionism: A Political Philosophy for Modern Times* (2014) | Examine role ethics and political institutions; test where relational arguments differ from familiar liberal and communitarian categories. |
| T26 | Michael Freeden, *Ideologies and Political Theory: A Conceptual Approach* (1996) | Investigate ideologies as configurations of political concepts and their relationships; use as a challenge to single-item identity labels. |
| T27 | Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651) | Examine security, conflict, authorization, and concentrated authority; distinguish a factual expectation of insecurity from endorsement of unlimited power. |

This is a purposive reading plan, not a balanced canon. It includes texts with incompatible arguments and historical limitations. Add contemporary criticism, scholarship from the intended languages and settings, and specific community scholarship where relevant. Do not fill a gap with a famous name alone.
